The problem

Growing your business shouldn't mean growing your headcount.

High-touch businesses depend on personal relationships, but the data connecting those relationships is scattered across systems, spreadsheets, emails and employees. From the first lead and referral through onboarding and service delivery, valuable information gets disconnected. Teams duplicate work, critical details fall through the cracks, and leadership lacks visibility across the business.

The result: growth requires more people, more manual effort and more complexity, instead of using AI to grow without adding headcount or losing the personal touch.

You can't see it without asking.

Which clients are at risk this week? Who hasn't been contacted in a month? What is each advocate's time going to? The answers exist, spread across five people and three tools, and getting them takes a meeting.

Things fall through the gaps.

A promise made on a call, a follow-up mentioned in a meeting, a family email waiting on a reply. Nobody dropped it on purpose. It just never became a task anyone could see.

Your best people do clerical work.

Every visit becomes a note, a task list, an email and a chat, typed again into each. Hours a week of re-entering what they already know, and when someone leaves, their knowledge leaves with them.

What changes

One record for every client, that sales, service and operations all work from. No handoff, no sync, no copy.

AI does the typing and the chasing. A sentence becomes the household, the tasks, the email and the chat. Inbound mail is triaged before anyone opens it.

A person confirms every action. Nothing reaches a record or a client unseen, and every correction teaches the system.

You see the whole business on one page, with every number clicking through to the record behind it.

What makes this different

The human validation is the product. The AI output is the draft.

Most AI products treat the model's answer as the finished work. We treat it as a proposal. A person confirms every write before it reaches a record, every edit and rejection carries a reason, and the system learns from those corrections. That stance, plus three habits, is the whole method.

Proposed, then confirmed

Nothing reaches a record unseen. The AI drafts the email, the task, the invoice line; your team approves it, and the difference between proposed and approved is the training signal.

The operating model is data

Roles, option lists, playbook steps and triage rules live in tables an admin edits. One deployment per customer stays affordable because changing the business never means changing the code.

The AI is the back end, not a plugin

A CRM with an AI add-on drafts faster into the same boxes. Here the AI reads each individual's Orbit, the people, conversations, commitments and history around them, decides what matters, and keeps that curated picture current. Sales, service and operations see the same picture from their own view.

Incidents become conventions

When something goes wrong, the fix is a written rule, a test or a guard, never an apology. Those rules, decision logs and runbooks are deliverables you keep.

What we took from it

That discipline can't live in one person's head. So every Note G engagement ships the judgment as written artifacts the client keeps: the data rules, the decision log, the UX standards, the handoff doc and the runbooks. The method is yours when we leave.

The standing rules

Every one of these was added after a real incident. That is what a convention is: an apology you only have to make once.

  1. No hardcoded lists. Every user-facing option comes from a table at runtime. A database row beats the code.
  2. No guessing. If the table structure or the business rule isn't known, the developer asks. A confident wrong answer is the most expensive kind.
  3. Mock before build. Anything with a visual shape is shown as a mockup and approved before a line is written.
  4. Complete files only. Every delivery is a whole file, never a patch, so what was reviewed is exactly what runs.
  5. Fixed deploy order. Migrations, then backend with a smoke check, then frontend. Every time, with the commands pre-filled.
  6. Reuse before rebuild. Find the existing component or path first. Two ways of doing the same thing is a bug waiting to be found.
  7. Stated facts beat inference. What a person entered outranks anything the AI inferred, in the data and in the code.
  8. No stale numbers. A dashboard that might be wrong rebuilds itself. A slow card is better than a confident lie.

What you keep

The full repository. The architecture document and decision log. The data rules, the UX standards and the deploy runbooks. The method is written down so that any engineer, human or AI, can pick it up. AI-written does not mean locked in.

What we check

Every AI write is proposed and confirmed, and the difference between proposed and executed is audited. Test and production are separate environments, nobody works in production, and every incident becomes a rule, a test or a guard.

Why "Note G"

The first algorithm was a note in the margin.

In 1843 Ada Lovelace appended seven notes to a paper on Babbage's Analytical Engine. The last of them, Note G, laid out step by step how the machine would compute Bernoulli numbers. It is widely regarded as the first published computer program.

She wrote it as a table of operations: which variables each step acts on, and what results. That is still the honest way to describe how a business runs, and it is how we design systems.

The Engine can do whatever we know how to order it to perform.Ada Lovelace, Note G, paraphrased

What we believe

Three rules we build to

A prompt is a request, not a constraint. If a rule matters, it is enforced in code and data, and tested. The model is asked nicely; the system makes sure.

Stated facts beat inference. A record a person entered outranks anything the AI guessed. Required fields are required, because bad data is forever.

The next step, not an error. When something is missing, the system asks a plain question and counts what's still needed. Nobody should decode a stack trace to finish their job.
